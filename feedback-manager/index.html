Why Feedback Fails

65% of employees want more feedback than they're currently getting — yet most managers avoid giving it, delay it, or deliver it in ways that backfire.

The result? Employees feel uncertain about their performance. Problems go unaddressed. Teams underperform. And new managers wonder why nothing is changing — not realizing that feedback is the missing lever.

This course gives you a simple, repeatable framework for delivering feedback that is specific, factual, and grounded in impact — not feelings or impressions.

The cost of avoiding feedback
👉
Top performers leave

Without recognition and growth feedback, strong employees look for opportunities elsewhere.

👉
Poor performance becomes the norm

Problems that go unaddressed become habits that are harder to break over time.

👉
Your credibility suffers

New managers who avoid hard conversations are seen as weak leaders — not kind ones.

The good news: effective feedback is a skill. And like any skill, it can be learned.

Five Common Feedback Mistakes

Most managers don't avoid feedback because they don't care — they avoid it because they don't have a reliable way to do it well. These are the five patterns that cause feedback to miss the mark.
💨

Too Vague

"Be more professional" — no actionable guidance, leaves the recipient unclear on what to change.

🕐

Delayed

Waiting for annual reviews lets issues persist and misses the window when feedback is most useful.

😡

Personal Criticism

Attacking character instead of behavior ("you don't care") triggers defensiveness and shuts down learning.

😂

The Feedback Sandwich

Burying critical feedback between excessive compliments dilutes the message and confuses the recipient.

🔈

Only Corrective

Feedback only when things go wrong creates anxiety and misses the chance to reinforce what's working.

Notice what all five share: they make feedback about impressions, timing failures, or personality — not about observable facts. The fix is a framework that keeps feedback grounded in evidence.

Section 3 of 7

Effective vs. Ineffective: Spot the Difference

The difference between feedback that works and feedback that backfires usually comes down to one word: specificity.

Effective feedback tells someone exactly what happened, why it mattered, and what the impact was. Ineffective feedback leaves people guessing — or defensive.

Compare these examples

✘ Ineffective

Vague
"You need to step it up."
Personal
"Your attitude has been off lately."
Meaningless positive
"You're doing fine — keep it up."
Judgment
"I feel like you're not fully committed."

✔ Effective

Specific + factual
"This report was submitted two days late without notice, which delayed the client deliverable."
Behavior-focused
"In yesterday's meeting, you interrupted two colleagues while they were presenting."
Meaningful positive
"You stayed late to help onboard the new hire — that's exactly the team behavior we need more of."

Notice the pattern in the effective examples: they always describe a specific situation, a specific behavior, and a specific impact. That's not an accident — it's the SBI framework you'll learn next.

The SBI Framework

SBI stands for Situation — Behavior — Impact. It gives you a clear, repeatable structure that removes guesswork and keeps feedback grounded in observable facts.

Select each component to learn what it means and how to use it.

S

Situation

When & where

B

Behavior

What they did

I

Impact

The effect

Situation — Set the specific context

Describe when and where the behavior occurred. Be precise — not "recently" or "sometimes," but "last Tuesday" or "in this morning's meeting."

Example"In this morning's team meeting..."    "Last Friday, when the client report was due..."

Behavior — Describe what you observed

Stick to observable facts — what the person said or did. No interpretations, no assumptions about intent. This is the hardest part: staying factual when you're frustrated.

Example"...you presented the project update without the data the team had requested..."    "...you submitted it four hours late without flagging the delay in advance..."

Impact — Explain the effect

Describe the real consequence on the team, the project, the customer, or the business. This is what makes feedback land — it connects the behavior to something that matters beyond the person's intentions.

Example"...which meant we couldn't make a decision on next steps and the meeting ran 20 minutes over."    "...which put our account team in an uncomfortable position with the client."
Full SBI statement — put it together
"In this morning's team meeting [S], you presented the project update without the data the team had requested [B], which meant we couldn't make a decision on next steps and the meeting ran 20 minutes over [I]."

SBI works for both corrective and positive feedback. Use it consistently — not just when something goes wrong.

Your Feedback Quick Reference

Keep this as your go-to reference before any feedback conversation.

📋 SBI Feedback Framework

S — Situation
Describe the specific context: when and where. "In Tuesday's client call..." / "Last week, when the proposal was due..."
B — Behavior
Describe observable facts only — what the person said or did. No interpretations. "You submitted the report without the financial analysis section."
I — Impact
Explain the real effect on the team, project, or customer. "...which meant the client couldn't approve the budget that day."
Five principles to keep in mind
Be SpecificObservable events, not general impressions.
Be TimelyWithin 24-48 hours of the event.
Focus on BehaviorActions, not personality traits.
Be BalancedUse SBI for positive feedback too, not just corrective.
Follow UpRevisit within 1-2 weeks to acknowledge progress.
Stay PrivateCorrective feedback: always one-on-one, never public.
