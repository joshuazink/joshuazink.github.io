Lesson 1 of 6

Why Most Training Fails

Your organization rolls out a new training program. Participation is high. Feedback is positive. Six months later, nothing had changed. Why?

Most corporate training fails not because the content is wrong, but because of structural design problems that prevent transfer to real job behavior. Understanding these patterns is the first step to designing differently.

Four Root Causes
The problem: Most corporate training is built on a model designed for children, where the instructor controls what, when, and how something is learned. Adults don't learn this way. They need relevance, autonomy, and connection to their own experience.

Design implication: Build from learners' real problems, not a content outline. Ask: why would someone care about this on the job?
The problem: Research shows less than 20% of training content is applied on the job six months later. A single session creates initial awareness; it rarely creates lasting behavior change.

Design implication: Design spaced reinforcement into the experience. One training event is not a learning program.
The problem: Even well-designed training fails if managers don't reinforce new behaviors on the job. When learners return to an environment that doesn't support change, old habits return.

Design implication: Build a manager guide. Include transfer strategies. L&D doesn't end at course completion.
The problem: If your quiz asks learners to recall definitions, you're measuring memory, not capability. Employees can pass and still be unprepared for the real job situation.

Design implication: Write knowledge checks at the application level. Use scenarios. Ask learners to make decisions, not recite facts.

"A single training event can create awareness. Only a system of spaced reinforcement and real-world application creates change."

Understanding Adult Learners: Knowles' Six Assumptions

Adults are not empty vessels. They arrive with experience, opinions, and a need to know why they're learning before they'll engage. Knowles' andragogy explains why this matters for design.
Flip each card to see the design implication
1
Self-Concept
Adults see themselves as self-directed. Design for choice: give learners control over sequence, depth, or application where possible.
2
Prior Experience
Adults' experience is a learning resource. Activate it: use reflection prompts and scenarios that connect to what they already know.
3
Readiness to Learn
Adults learn what they need to know right now. Stage content when it aligns with a real transition or role responsibility.
4
Problem-Centered
Adults want to solve problems, not cover topics. Open with the problem before introducing any framework or concept.
5
Intrinsic Motivation
Adults are motivated by internal payoffs: career growth, job effectiveness. Make the "why" explicit at the start of every module.
6
Need to Know
Adults want to understand why before they'll commit to learning something. Justify every lesson, not just the course as a whole.

Lesson 3 of 6

Cognitive Load: Designing Within the Brain's Limits

Working memory can only hold about 4 items at once. Every design decision either respects that limit or blows past it, causing learners to shut down.

Cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) identifies three types of mental effort a learner experiences. Good instructional design reduces extraneous load and manages intrinsic load so learners can focus on what matters.

Three Types of Cognitive Load

Intrinsic load is the inherent complexity of the content itself. Some topics are just harder than others; you can't eliminate this load, but you can manage it through sequencing and chunking.

Design strategyStart with foundational concepts before layered ones. Use worked examples to reduce the initial demand on a learner's working memory.

Extraneous load is mental effort caused by poor design: cluttered slides, redundant information, unnecessary navigation friction. This is the load you control entirely.

Design strategyRemove decorative visuals that add no meaning. Avoid reading slides aloud. Don't make learners hunt for what matters; put it front and center.

Germane load is the productive mental effort that leads to learning: connecting new information to existing knowledge, building mental schemas, making meaning.

Design strategyUse analogies, case studies, and reflection prompts that encourage learners to actively connect new content to their own experience.

Lesson 4 of 6

The Forgetting Curve & Spaced Repetition

Without reinforcement, learners forget roughly 50% of new information within a day, and up to 80% within a week. This isn't a motivation problem. It's a design problem.

Hermann Ebbinghaus mapped this decay in the 1880s. The curve hasn't changed. What has changed is our ability to design around it.

🚨
Without reinforcement

50% forgotten within 24 hrs. Less than 20% applied on the job after 6 months.

📈
With spaced review

Each review resets retention and strengthens the long-term memory trace.

⏱
Optimal spacing

Review at 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, then 1 month. Each interval can grow longer.

🛠
Design implication

Build follow-up touchpoints into every program. Email reminders, job aids, and manager check-ins all count.

Kirkpatrick's Four Levels of Evaluation

If you can only measure whether learners liked the training, you can't prove it worked. Kirkpatrick's framework gives L&D professionals a language for measuring what actually matters.

Select each level to see what it measures and how to collect it.

1

Reaction

2

Learning

3

Behavior

4

Results

Level 1: Reaction — Did learners find it relevant and engaging?

Reaction measures how participants felt about the training. It's the most common level measured and the least predictive of actual learning or behavior change.

How to measure
  • Post-course satisfaction surveys
  • Net Promoter Score for training
  • Engagement metrics (completion, time-on-task)

Level 2: Learning — Did knowledge, skills, or attitudes change?

Learning measures whether participants actually acquired what the training intended to teach, not just whether they finished it.

How to measure
  • Pre/post assessments (not just post)
  • Skill demonstrations or role plays
  • Knowledge checks written at application level

Level 3: Behavior — Are they applying it on the job?

Behavior is the hardest level to measure and the most meaningful for L&D. It requires follow-up observation, manager input, or 360 data, typically 30 to 90 days post-training.

How to measure
  • Manager observation checklists
  • 360-degree feedback
  • Follow-up surveys at 30, 60, 90 days

Level 4: Results — Did it move the business metric?

Results connect training to outcomes the business already cares about: sales, quality, retention, safety. This level requires baseline data collected before training begins.

How to measure
  • Sales revenue, error rates, customer scores
  • Retention and turnover data
  • Comparison against a control group where possible
