Lesson 1 of 5  ·  Introduction: The Situation

Meet Jordan

You are Alex Rivera, a manager on a client services team. Your direct report, Jordan, missed the Meridian client deliverable last Friday. The client noticed. Your director noticed. Now you need to have a conversation.

Jordan has been on your team for two years. This is the first time a deadline has slipped. Their track record is strong. Before you say anything, you need to decide: how are you going to approach this conversation?

Lesson 1 of 5  ·  Knowledge Check
Before you meet with Jordan, how do you prepare?
A
Pull Jordan's recent work, note this is a first offense, and schedule a private 1:1 to understand what happened.
B
Send Jordan an email asking them to explain in writing what happened with the Meridian deliverable.
C
Walk over to Jordan's desk and ask them directly what happened in front of the rest of the team.
Correct
Good instinct. Reviewing the context first helps you enter the conversation informed rather than reactive. Scheduling a private conversation protects Jordan's dignity and sets a coaching tone before the meeting starts.
Not quite
That approach skips the preparation and the private setting that a coaching conversation requires. A coaching mindset begins before the conversation does -- with context, not confrontation.
Lesson 2 of 5  ·  The Opening: Starting the Conversation

The Meeting Begins

It is 10 AM. Jordan walks into your office and takes a seat. You can tell they are tense. They know why they are here.

This is the first time you have had to address a performance issue with Jordan. This is not a disciplinary conversation. It is a coaching conversation. The difference is everything: your tone, your opening question, and the direction you take from here.

How you open this conversation will determine whether Jordan feels safe enough to tell you the truth.

Lesson 2 of 5  ·  Knowledge Check
You want to start with curiosity, not conclusions. How do you open the conversation with Jordan?
A
Start with a calm, open-ended question: "I wanted to check in with you about the Meridian deliverable. Can you walk me through what happened on Friday?"
B
Jump straight to the problem: "The client is upset and my director is on my case. What went wrong with the Meridian deliverable?"
C
Open with an explanation of consequences: "I need to tell you that this missed deadline is now on your record and could affect your performance rating."
Correct
Well done. Starting with a genuine, open-ended question invites Jordan to share rather than defend, and establishes a coaching tone from the start.
Not quite
That approach is likely to put Jordan on the defensive. Effective coaching conversations start with curiosity and an open question, not urgency or consequences.
Lesson 3 of 5  ·  The Core: Uncovering What Happened

Digging Deeper

Jordan opens up. They explain that Friday was chaotic: three competing deadlines, an emergency call from another stakeholder, and the Meridian deliverable slipped. Jordan looks frustrated with themselves.

You can tell this is not a pattern. It is a moment.

Now the real coaching begins. What do you do with what you just heard?

Lesson 3 of 5  ·  Knowledge Check
Jordan says: "I honestly just got pulled in too many directions at once. I'm really sorry." How do you respond?
A
Acknowledge what you heard and ask a coaching question: "It sounds like you were dealing with a lot at once. What do you think could have helped you catch this earlier?"
B
Move to problem-solving immediately: "Okay, so next time just drop everything else and focus on the top priority deliverable until it is done."
C
Reassure and close: "I appreciate you being honest. It happens. Just make sure this doesn't happen again and we'll move on."
Correct
Good instinct. Acknowledging first shows you heard Jordan, then asking a coaching question helps develop self-awareness and ownership rather than just a promise to do better.
Not quite
Jumping to solutions or minimizing the situation skips the coaching moment. Reflect back what you heard, then invite Jordan to think through it with you.
Lesson 4 of 5  ·  The Path Forward: Commitment and Close

Moving Forward Together

The conversation is shifting. Jordan has reflected, taken ownership, and is now engaged. You have acknowledged what happened and explored the root cause.

Now it is time to close the loop: agree on a specific path forward, reinforce your confidence in Jordan, and leave them feeling supported.

How you close this conversation matters as much as how you opened it.

Lesson 4 of 5  ·  Knowledge Check
Jordan has reflected and taken responsibility. You are ready to close the conversation. What is the best way to wrap up?
A
Agree on a specific action together: "Let's put a standing flag in place so if you're ever stretched thin again, you reach out before a deadline slips. Does that work for you?" Then affirm Jordan's ownership and close with confidence.
B
End with a warning: "I'm glad we talked, but I need you to understand that another miss like this will have real consequences for your standing on the team."
C
Keep it brief: "Okay, good talk. Just make sure it doesn't happen again. You're dismissed."
Correct
Exactly. Closing with a concrete agreement and a genuine affirmation reinforces accountability without undermining trust. The conversation ends on a note of partnership.
Not quite
A coaching conversation that ends with a threat or a dismissal loses the ground you gained. The close should reinforce ownership and confidence, not fear.
Lesson 5 of 5  ·  The Outcome

What You Did Well

You navigated this conversation the way a skilled coach does: with curiosity before conclusions, context before consequences, and commitment before closure.

You opened with a question, not a verdict. You listened before you led. You helped Jordan think rather than just telling them what to do. And you closed with a specific agreement and a clear expression of confidence.

That is what coaching leadership looks like in practice.

The conversation with Jordan is over. But the skills you practiced here apply to every difficult conversation you will lead from here. The more you lead with curiosity, the stronger your relationships and your team will become.

Course Complete

You practiced the full arc of a coaching conversation:

  • Prepared with context before the conversation
  • Opened with curiosity, not conclusions
  • Listened before leading
  • Closed with a specific commitment and affirmation

Every difficult conversation you lead from here is an opportunity to build trust while holding people accountable. Those two things are not opposites -- they are the point.
